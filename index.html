01

Coverage means nothing here.

A modern robot doesn't run hand-written control code. It runs a neural network trained in simulation — a policy — mapping what the robot senses to what its motors do. It works under the conditions it was trained on and offers no guarantee anywhere else, and nothing in it signals when a situation is unfamiliar.

That breaks every instinct from software testing. Ordinary programs have branches you can enumerate, so "100% coverage" means something. A network has none. Line coverage of the inference code is 100% after a single run and tells you nothing. The space of situations is a continuous volume, not a list of cases — and a hundred scripted scenarios tell you about a hundred points in it.

03

A failure is a rule you wrote.

Never a learned classifier, never a heuristic we invented. When a run is flagged you can read the line that flagged it and disagree with it. Checked at every step of the whole trajectory, not just the final state.

predicates:
  - {name: tilt_limit, signal: tilt_deg,  op: ">", threshold: 35.0}
  - {name: fallen,     signal: height_m,  op: "<", threshold: 0.12}

Predicates see four signals and nothing else: tilt_deg, height_m, contact_force_n, joint_vel_rads.

05

What we can show, and what we can't.

  • Can — name the conditions under which a policy broke, reduce each to its smallest form, and hand over an archive where every run re-executes bit-for-bit from its seed.
  • Cannot — show that failures are absent. The search finds them; it can never prove there are none left.
  • Will not — call a policy safe, verified or compliant, or imply the output satisfies a notified body. Nobody can currently say what does.
  • Today — one robot class exercised, no completed customer pilots, one person. EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 applies 20 January 2027; the delegated act defining adequate evidence is not due until 2028.
